\section{Where the Last Two Lectures Ran Out}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Where the Last Two Lectures Ran Out}{%
  Two architectures, two different ways of failing to see time.}

% --- Slide: the gap ----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Two Architectures That Cannot See Time}
  Lecture 7 gave you a feedforward network. It treats each row independently --- everything it
  knows about \emph{when} something happened, you encoded by hand as a lag column. Shuffle the
  features and the model is unchanged.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  Lecture 8 gave you a 1D CNN, which does see local shape. But its \textbf{receptive field} is
  fixed by architecture: stacked width-5 and width-3 filters reach about seven weeks back, and no
  reasonable stack of small filters reaches an annual lag. Worse, pooling deliberately discarded
  \emph{where} in the window each pattern occurred --- and we measured that costing about 500 RMSE.

  \begin{keybox}
    Both architectures share one assumption: the input is a \textbf{fixed-size block} to be
    processed all at once. Neither has any notion of reading a sequence \emph{in order} and
    carrying something forward.
  \end{keybox}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  That is the gap today closes.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: the recurrent idea -----------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{The Recurrent Idea: Carry State Forward}
  A recurrent network reads the sequence one step at a time and maintains a \textbf{hidden state}
  $\mathbf{h}_t$ --- a running summary of everything seen so far.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  At each step it does two things: update the summary using the new input \emph{and} the previous
  summary, then optionally emit an output. The same weights are used at every step, which is
  weight sharing again --- along time rather than along space.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{The consequence worth sitting with.} There is no fixed window. In principle
  $\mathbf{h}_t$ encodes the entire history $x_1, \ldots, x_t$, so the architecture imposes no
  limit on how far back the model can look.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{In principle.} Most of this lecture is about the gap between that promise and what a
  recurrent network actually delivers --- and about the architecture built to close it.
\end{frame}

% =============================================================================
\section{The Vanilla RNN}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{The Vanilla RNN}{%
  One equation, applied over and over --- and the reason
  that is both the whole idea and the whole problem.}

% --- Slide: RNN mechanics ----------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Vanilla RNN: One Equation, Repeated}
  \begin{definitionbox}{The recurrence}
    \[
      \mathbf{h}_t = \tanh\!\left(\mathbf{W}_h \mathbf{h}_{t-1} + \mathbf{W}_x \mathbf{x}_t + \mathbf{b}\right),
      \qquad
      \hat{y}_t = \mathbf{W}_y \mathbf{h}_t + \mathbf{b}_y
    \]
    $\mathbf{W}_h$, $\mathbf{W}_x$, $\mathbf{W}_y$ are the \emph{same} matrices at every step.
  \end{definitionbox}

  Read the first equation in words: the new summary is a squashed mix of the old summary and the
  new observation. That is the entire model.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  Because the weights are shared across steps, a 26-week window costs no more parameters than a
  5-week one --- and the same network handles sequences of different lengths without modification.
  Our RNN below holds about \textbf{4{,}500 parameters}, against 22{,}657 for the small
  feedforward network in Lecture 7.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  For forecasting we usually want one number, not a sequence, so we take the \textbf{final}
  hidden state $\mathbf{h}_T$ --- the summary after reading the whole window --- and put one
  linear layer on top of it.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: BPTT -------------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Training It: Backpropagation Through Time}
  Training an RNN is ordinary backpropagation applied to an unusual graph. \textbf{Unroll} the
  recurrence across the window and it becomes a 26-layer feedforward network --- one layer per
  time step --- in which every layer shares the same weights.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  Gradients then flow backward from the loss at step $T$ all the way to step 1. Because
  $\mathbf{W}_h$ appears at every step, its gradient is a \emph{sum} of contributions from all 26
  positions.

  \begin{warningbox}
    \textbf{This is where it breaks.} Propagating a gradient back through $k$ steps multiplies by
    roughly $\mathbf{W}_h$ $k$ times. If the relevant factor is below 1, the gradient shrinks
    \emph{exponentially}; above 1, it explodes. Either way, long-range signal does not survive
    the trip.
  \end{warningbox}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  Exploding gradients have an easy fix --- \texttt{clip\_grad\_norm\_} caps the size of the
  update, and we use it. Vanishing gradients do not, which is what the next section is for.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: vanishing gradients ----------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Why a Vanilla RNN Forgets \parencite{Bengio1994}}
  Put numbers on it. Suppose each backward step multiplies the gradient by a factor of $0.7$ ---
  an unremarkable value for a $\tanh$ network.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{rrl}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Steps back} & \textbf{Gradient factor} & \textbf{Meaning} \\
      \midrule
      1  & $0.70$        & full signal \\
      5  & $0.17$        & weakened but usable \\
      10 & $0.028$       & barely present \\
      26 & $0.000\,002$  & gone \\
      52 & $\approx 10^{-9}$ & the annual lag, invisible \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{The promise from two slides ago fails in practice.} A vanilla RNN \emph{can} in
  principle encode arbitrarily long history, but the gradient that would teach it to do so has
  vanished long before it reaches back that far. In practice these networks learn dependencies of
  roughly ten steps.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{Bengio and colleagues} proved this is not an optimization bug to be tuned away --- it
  is inherent to the architecture. Which is why the fix had to be a different architecture.
\end{frame}

\section{LSTM: Long Short-Term Memory}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{LSTM: Long Short-Term Memory}{%
  Add a memory that information passes through rather than
  being recomputed at every step.}

% --- Slide: LSTM gates -------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{LSTM: A Cell State and Three Gates \parencite{Hochreiter1997}}
  An LSTM carries \textbf{two} things forward: a \textbf{cell state} $\mathbf{c}_t$, which is the
  long-term memory, and a \textbf{hidden state} $\mathbf{h}_t$, which is what the rest of the
  network sees. Three gates control the traffic between them.
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{2.3cm}p{10.2cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Forget gate} & $\mathbf{f}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_f[\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_f)$
        --- what fraction of the old memory to keep \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Input gate} & $\mathbf{i}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_i[\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_i)$
        --- how much of the new candidate to write \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Output gate} & $\mathbf{o}_t = \sigma(\mathbf{W}_o[\mathbf{h}_{t-1}, \mathbf{x}_t] + \mathbf{b}_o)$
        --- how much of memory to expose \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{-0.1cm}
  The memory update itself is the line that matters:
  \[
    \mathbf{c}_t = \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} \;+\; \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t,
    \qquad
    \mathbf{h}_t = \mathbf{o}_t \odot \tanh(\mathbf{c}_t)
  \]
  \vspace{-0.3cm}

  Each gate is a sigmoid, so it outputs between 0 and 1 --- a soft switch the network learns.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: gates in business terms -------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{What the Gates Are Doing, in Plain Terms}
  The equations are compact but the behavior is intuitive. Take a store forecasting demand week
  by week.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{p{2.4cm}p{10.1cm}}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Forget} & ``The promotion that ran three months ago no longer explains anything ---
      drop it.'' The gate closes toward 0 and that part of memory decays away. \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Input} & ``This week is Thanksgiving and the spike is enormous --- record it.'' The
      gate opens toward 1 and the event is written into the cell. \\[3pt]
      \textbf{Output} & ``I am holding a memory of last December, but this week is April --- do
      not act on it yet.'' Information stays in $\mathbf{c}_t$ without contaminating the current
      prediction. \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{The output gate is the one people skip, and it is the subtle one.} It separates
  \emph{what the model remembers} from \emph{what the model uses right now}. A vanilla RNN cannot
  make that distinction --- its single hidden state has to serve both jobs at once, so anything
  it stores is immediately in play.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: why it fixes the gradient -----------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Why the Cell State Fixes the Gradient}
  Look again at the memory update, and specifically at the operation in the middle:
  \[
    \mathbf{c}_t = \mathbf{f}_t \odot \mathbf{c}_{t-1} + \mathbf{i}_t \odot \tilde{\mathbf{c}}_t
  \]
  \vspace{-0.3cm}

  The old memory enters \textbf{additively}, and it is not passed through a weight matrix or a
  $\tanh$ on the way. When the forget gate is near 1, $\mathbf{c}_t \approx \mathbf{c}_{t-1}$
  plus whatever is new --- and the gradient flows backward through that addition essentially
  undiminished.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  Compare with the vanilla RNN, where every backward step multiplies by $\mathbf{W}_h$ and the
  $\tanh$ derivative. Repeated multiplication shrinks; repeated addition does not.

  \begin{keybox}
    \textbf{You have seen this fix before.} It is the ResNet skip connection from Lecture 8 ---
    $\mathbf{y} = \mathcal{F}(\mathbf{x}) + \mathbf{x}$ --- solving the same problem in the same
    way. Give the gradient an additive path home and depth stops destroying it.
  \end{keybox}

  LSTM (1997) predates ResNet (2015) by eighteen years --- the idea was rediscovered for depth
  after being invented for time.
\end{frame}

\section{In PyTorch, and Does It Work}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{In PyTorch, and Does It Work}{%
  Four lines of model code, and an honest measurement against
  everything else this course has fitted.}

% --- Slide: PyTorch ----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{LSTM for Forecasting in PyTorch}
  \begin{examplebox}{Window in, one forecast out}
    {\tiny\linespread{1.2}\selectfont
    \texttt{class LSTMForecaster(nn.Module):}\\
    \texttt{\ \ def \_\_init\_\_(self, n\_features, hidden=64, layers=1):}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ super().\_\_init\_\_()}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ self.lstm = nn.LSTM(n\_features, hidden, num\_layers=layers,}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ batch\_first=True)}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ self.fc = nn.Linear(hidden, 1)}\\[3pt]
    \texttt{\ \ def forward(self, x):\ \ \ \ \ \ \# x: (batch, seq\_len, n\_features)}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ out, (h, c) = self.lstm(x)}\\
    \texttt{\ \ \ \ return self.fc(out[:, -1, :]).squeeze(-1)}
    }
  \end{examplebox}
  \texttt{batch\_first=True} gives shape \texttt{(batch, seq\_len, features)} --- the
  \emph{opposite} of \texttt{Conv1d}. \texttt{out[:, -1, :]} is the state after the last step.
  Lecture 7's rules still hold, plus \texttt{clip\_grad\_norm\_(..., 1.0)}.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: results ----------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Does It Work? The Scoreboard So Far}
  Same weekly panel, same 26-week windows, same test block as Homework 4.
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lrr}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Model} & \textbf{Test RMSE} & \textbf{Parameters} \\
      \midrule
      LASSO, 46 engineered features & \textbf{744} & 46 \\
      XGBoost, 46 engineered features & 781 & --- \\
      \textbf{Vanilla RNN}, 4 raw channels & \textbf{842} & 4{,}545 \\
      Random forest & 899 & --- \\
      \textbf{LSTM}, 4 raw channels & 987 & 17{,}985 \\
      1D CNN, best variant & 1{,}032 & 8{,}161 \\
      Seasonal naive & 2{,}152 & 0 \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{-0.05cm}
  \textbf{Recurrence beats convolution decisively} --- and it did so on \emph{four raw channels}
  with no lag engineering at all, against 46 hand-built features for LASSO. That is the case for
  reading a sequence in order, in one line.

  \vspace{0.1cm}
  \textbf{And the LSTM lost to the vanilla RNN} --- at a fixed 30-epoch budget. That is not a
  typo, and the next three slides are about what it does and does not mean.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: why LSTM lost ----------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Why the Gated Model Lost at 30 Epochs}
  The result holds at all three window lengths --- but all three share one panel and one split,
  so this is one finding measured three ways, not three independent tests:
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lrrl}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Window} & \textbf{Vanilla RNN} & \textbf{LSTM} & \\
      \midrule
      13 weeks & \textbf{878} & 990 & RNN wins \\
      26 weeks & \textbf{842} & 987 & RNN wins \\
      52 weeks & \textbf{805} & 998 & RNN wins \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{-0.3cm}
  \textbf{An LSTM solves a problem this data does not have} --- gating preserves information
  across \emph{long} chains, and weekly demand is dominated by the recent past. The gates are not
  free: \textbf{17{,}985 parameters against 4{,}545}. Before concluding they bought nothing,
  this table owes two checks: \textbf{was the comparison fair}, and \textbf{what in the data
  explains it}.

  \begin{warningbox}
    A margin measured at one training budget is a claim about that budget. 30 epochs was the
    default, not a setting shown to be enough for both models.
  \end{warningbox}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: the budget check -------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Check 1: Was It a Fair Fight?}
  An LSTM carries four times the parameters. Bigger models converge slower. So vary the
  \textbf{training budget} and watch the margin, at the 26-week window:
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Epochs} & \textbf{10} & \textbf{30} & \textbf{60} & \textbf{100} \\
      \midrule
      Vanilla RNN & 1{,}099 & \textbf{842} & 828 & 830 \\
      LSTM & 1{,}002 & 987 & 926 & \textbf{821} \\
      \midrule
      Gap (LSTM $-$ RNN) & $-97$ & $+145$ & $+98$ & $-9$ \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{-0.1cm}
  The gap is \textbf{largest at exactly the budget we used}, and it closes at every checkpoint
  after it. The RNN is flat from about epoch 45; the LSTM is \emph{still improving} at 100 --- we
  ran out of patience, not the model.

  \begin{keybox}
    At a fixed 30-epoch budget the RNN wins by a wide margin. At convergence the two
    \textbf{tie}. The RNN's genuine advantage is that it gets there roughly \textbf{three times
    faster on a quarter of the parameters} --- an optimization advantage, not a verdict on gating.
  \end{keybox}
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: the data check ---------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Check 2: What in the Data Explains It}
  Gating pays when a value must survive many steps that would otherwise overwrite it. Three
  measurements say this panel has no such structure.

  \vspace{0.08cm}
  {\small
  \textbf{1. Most variance is not sequential.} \textbf{91\%} of the variation in weekly units is
  \emph{between} series --- which store, which category. The main task is reading the level, and
  the last few weeks give you that.

  \vspace{0.06cm}
  \textbf{2. The series are smooth.} Within-series autocorrelation is \textbf{0.83} at lag 1 and
  \textbf{0.43} at lag 52 --- so the recent past is a \emph{sufficient statistic} for the distant
  past. Nothing must be carried across a gap, because the gap was filled in continuously.

  \vspace{0.06cm}
  \textbf{3. Nothing arrives early.} \texttt{snap\_days} correlates $+0.14$ with units in the
  same week and $\approx 0$ at every lead; \texttt{event\_days} is flat everywhere. No
  \emph{announcement} structure --- precisely what a forget gate is for.
  }

  \begin{keybox}
    {\small Ask these of your own series before reaching for a gated model: how much of my
    variance is cross-sectional? Do recent values already summarize old ones? Does anything in my
    inputs \textbf{lead} the target?}
  \end{keybox}
\end{frame}

\section{Key Takeaways}
% =============================================================================

\sectionslide{Key Takeaways}{%
  What recurrence buys, what it costs, and what is still missing.}

% --- Slide: comparison -------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{RNN and LSTM in Context}
  \begin{center}
    {\small
    \begin{tabular}{llll}
      \toprule
      \textbf{Architecture} & \textbf{Sees order?} & \textbf{Effective memory} & \textbf{Cost} \\
      \midrule
      FFN on lags & no & whatever you engineered & cheap \\
      1D CNN & local only & receptive field ($\sim$7 steps) & cheap \\
      Vanilla RNN & yes & $\sim$10--30 steps in practice & cheap \\
      LSTM & yes & hundreds of steps & 4$\times$ an RNN \\
      \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
    }
  \end{center}

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  An LSTM holds roughly \textbf{four times} the parameters of a vanilla RNN at the same hidden
  size --- each of the three gates plus the candidate needs its own weight matrix. That is the
  price of the memory, and on a short window you pay it for nothing.

  \vspace{0.15cm}
  \textbf{What is still missing.} An LSTM reads strictly left to right, one step at a time, so
  it cannot be parallelized across the sequence, and everything it knows about step 3 must
  survive being squeezed through every state between 3 and $T$. Part 2 removes both constraints.
\end{frame}

% --- Slide: takeaways --------------------------------------------------------
\begin{frame}{Lecture 9 Part 1: Key Takeaways}
  \begin{keybox}
    {\footnotesize
    \begin{enumerate}\itemsep2pt
      \item A \textbf{recurrent network} reads a sequence in order and carries a hidden state.
            Weights are shared across time, so window length costs no parameters.

      \item Training is \textbf{backpropagation through time}: unroll the recurrence and it is a
            deep network whose layers share weights. Repeated multiplication shrinks the
            gradient exponentially --- at 52 steps, around $10^{-9}$.

      \item An \textbf{LSTM} adds a cell state updated \textbf{additively}. Addition preserves
            gradients where multiplication destroys them --- the ResNet fix, eighteen years
            earlier.

      \item Three gates: \textbf{forget} what is stale, \textbf{input} what is new,
            \textbf{output} what is relevant now.

      \item On our panel both recurrent models \textbf{beat every CNN variant} on four raw
            channels --- and both still lose to a 46-coefficient LASSO.

      \item The \textbf{LSTM lost to the vanilla RNN at a 30-epoch budget}, at all three window
            lengths --- but the two \textbf{tie} once both reach convergence, and 91\% of this
            panel's variance is cross-sectional anyway. \textbf{Fit the architecture to the
            problem --- and check a margin survives the budget you measured it at.}
    \end{enumerate}
    }
  \end{keybox}
\end{frame}
